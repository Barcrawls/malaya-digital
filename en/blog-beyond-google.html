Beyond Google: Bing, Apple Maps and the platforms your business should be on (that nobody tells you about)

By  Ciro Jaén  · Founder of MALAYA · Operator of experience businesses in Panama and Colombia
Direct answer: your customers don't search for you only on Google. iPhone users search on Apple Maps and ask Siri; AI assistants pull data from Bing and the open web; and every industry has its own decision platforms. Being only on Google means leaving doors closed — and opening them is free in almost every case.

Why does everyone talk only about Google?

Because it's the dominant search engine — and rightly the place to start. But "dominant" doesn't mean "only": a real share of your customers decide on other screens, and those platforms have far less competition precisely because almost no business bothers to be well set up there. It's cheap visibility waiting for someone to claim it.

Bing and Bing Places: the gateway to artificial intelligence

Bing is Microsoft's search engine — built into Windows, into the Edge browser, and the one feeding data to several AI assistants. When someone asks an assistant "what to do in Panama at night?", part of that answer is built with information indexed by Bing.

registering on Bing Places takes minutes (it can import your Google profile directly) and positions you in the fastest-growing channel: AI-generated answers. The business with consistent data and solid reviews is the one the AI cites.

Apple Maps and Apple Business Connect: where iPhones live

iPhone users — a huge share of the tourism Panama receives — search "near me" on Apple Maps, ask Siri, and see suggestions in Spotlight without ever touching Google. Apple Business Connect is the free tool to control that listing: photos, hours, booking links.

if you don't claim your listing, Apple shows third-party data — sometimes old or wrong. A wrong schedule on Apple Maps costs a business real customers without the owner ever finding out.

Your industry's platforms: where the purchase gets decided

Every sector has its decision venues. In tourism and experiences: TripAdvisor, Viator, GetYourGuide, Airbnb Experiences. In dining: the guides and booking apps. In lodging: the OTAs. It's not about being everywhere — it's about being impeccable on the ones your customer uses to decide, with the same information, the same photos and the same story on each one.

The silent requirement: total consistency

Here's the detail that ties it all together: these platforms read each other, and search engines and AIs compare your data across sources. Name, address, phone and hours identical everywhere is what builds the algorithmic trust that makes you show up. An inconsistent ecosystem doesn't add presence: it subtracts credibility.

And a warning of principle: all of this works only with each platform's official practices. No duplicate profiles, fabricated reviews or ranking tricks — shortcuts get detected, and the penalty ranges from losing visibility to getting wiped out in one stroke. What we build must be able to withstand any review, today and in five years.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth being on Bing if everyone uses Google?

Yes, and increasingly so: Bing's data feeds AI assistants and the searches built into Windows. Registration takes minutes and imports your Google profile.

What is Apple Business Connect?

Apple's free tool to control how you appear on Apple Maps, Siri and Spotlight — what iPhone users see when they search nearby.

Do AI assistants recommend businesses?

Yes, using information from the open web and search engines like Bing. A business with consistent data, solid reviews and clear content is more likely to get cited.

How many platforms should I be on?

The ones your customer uses to decide: Google and Apple Maps almost always, Bing for its weight in AI, and your industry's platforms. A few well-maintained beat many abandoned.
